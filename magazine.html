01 · Editor's Note

Why we chose the rain

By Ishita, editor for this issue

When our class teacher asked us to pick a theme for August, someone said "festivals" and someone said "space". Then Rohan said "rain", and the whole room went quiet for a second, because it was raining right then and we had all been listening to it without noticing.

That is the thing about the monsoon in Ghatshila. It is not an event. It arrives and then it is simply the weather, for weeks, until the ground gives up and the Subarnarekha turns brown and heavy and everyone's shoes are ruined.

We decided the whole issue would be about it. Not about how rain is good for farmers — we have all written that essay — but about what it actually looks like here, in this town, this year.

What we collected was better than we expected. A poem about the first shower. A set of photographs from the riverbank. An interview with the man who looks after our school garden, who turns out to know more about the monsoon than any of us. And Sneha's paintings, which we put at the end because we wanted the issue to finish quietly.

The September theme will be announced next week. Send your work to any of the editors before the 25th.

02 · Poem

The Smell of the First Shower

By Yamir, VII-B
For eleven days the fan turned
and moved nothing but the heat around.
The mango leaves hung like tired flags.
The dog under the water tank did not get up.
Then at four in the afternoon
the light went the colour of weak tea
and the crows stopped arguing
all at once, as if told to.
The smell came before the water did.
Wet dust. Hot stone cooling.
A smell with no name in English
that everyone in the house recognised
and nobody bothered to mention.
My grandmother took the washing in.
My mother took the washing in.
I stood in the courtyard and did nothing useful
and let it start on my face.

04 · Interview

An Interview with Our Gardener

By Ananya and Priya, VII-B · Conducted in the school garden, 24 July

Bishnu Mahato has looked after the plants at our school for nineteen years. We found him near the hibiscus bed with a bucket and asked whether he had ten minutes. He said he had five. We got twenty.

Is the monsoon the easiest time of year for you?
He laughed at this for a while. He said everyone thinks so, because he does not have to water anything. But he said the rain does his watering and then it does about four other things he did not ask for.
Such as?
Weeds, mainly. He said in the hot months a weed takes two weeks to become a problem. In the monsoon it takes three days. He also said the soil washes off the raised beds and has to be pushed back by hand, and that the grass in front of the office grows faster than he can cut it.
Which plants in the school do best in the rain?
The hibiscus, he said immediately, and the ferns near the water tank that nobody planted on purpose. He said the marigolds hate it and half of them rot at the stem every year, which is why the flowers for the Independence Day assembly are always bought and not grown.
Is there anything students do that makes your work harder?
He was polite about this. He said walking across the wet beds is the main one — the soil compacts and then nothing grows there for months. He said he does not mind the footballs.
Nineteen years. Has the monsoon changed?
He thought about this longer than any other question. He said it used to start in the second week of June, almost to the day, and now it can be two or three weeks late. He said when it comes it comes harder — more water in fewer days. He said he plants the same things at the same time as always and some years it works.

We asked what he would plant if the school gave him a free hand. He said a jamun tree, near the back wall, and then said the students would strip it in a week. He did not seem to mind that either.
